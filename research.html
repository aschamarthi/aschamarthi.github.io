Research

Numerical methods for high-speed compressible flows

My current research develops high-order numerical methods that exploit the mathematical and characteristic structure of the compressible-flow governing equations. Across shock-dominated, turbulent, multicomponent, multiphase, and real-gas regimes, the objective is to control numerical dissipation, preserve physically distinct wave families, and translate these advances into robust GPU-accelerated solvers.

01

A reusable numerical framework

Gradient-Based Reconstruction for Compressible Flow Simulation

The next stage developed Gradient-Based Reconstruction for compressible flow. Derivative information is used to reconstruct flow variables accurately, while the same gradients can be shared between inviscid and viscous flux calculations.

This creates a compact, modular route to high-order Navier–Stokes solvers. The framework was applied to single-component and multicomponent problems containing shocks and turbulent features, and was competitive with established WENO-family approaches while reducing reconstruction cost.

Representative work: Gradient Based Reconstruction (Computers & Fluids, 2023); Efficient High-Order Gradient-Based Reconstruction (JCP, 2023).

02

Stability beyond formal order

Importance of Viscous Flux Discretization

Viscous discretization is often overshadowed by inviscid shock-capturing design. My work showed that its spectral properties—especially high-frequency damping—can control stability and accuracy on marginally resolved grids.

This explains why nominally high-order operators may still perform poorly for shocks, turbulence, and under-resolved structures, while differently designed operators with favorable spectral behavior can be more reliable. The result is a practical design basis for viscous fluxes in high-speed turbulent-flow solvers.

Representative work: On the Importance of High-Frequency Damping… (JCP, 2022); On the Role of Spectral Properties… (Computers & Fluids, 2023).

03

Treating waves by their physics

Wave-Appropriate Methods for High-Speed Compressible Flows

Wave-appropriate methods use the characteristic structure of the Euler equations to distinguish acoustic, entropy, and vortical waves. Instead of applying one reconstruction strategy to every feature, the method assigns dissipation and reconstruction according to each wave family’s physical behavior.

This reduces avoidable damping of smooth vortical and turbulent structures while maintaining stability near shocks and contacts. In wall-modeled large-eddy simulation, the approach enabled coarse-grid prediction of hypersonic boundary-layer transition and key skin-friction and heat-transfer trends.

Representative work: A Wave Appropriate Discontinuity Sensor Approach (Physics of Fluids, 2023); Centralized Gradient-Based Reconstruction for WMLES… (JCP, 2024); Physics-Constrained Acoustic Dissipation and Rank-1 Entropy Wave Correction (JCP, 2026).

04

From single fluids to material interfaces

Physics-Consistent Reconstruction for Multicomponent and Multiphase Flows

At Caltech, I extended physics-consistent reconstruction to viscous compressible multicomponent and multiphase flows. These systems combine shocks, material interfaces, contacts, and vortical structures—features that require distinct numerical treatment.

The resulting algorithms apply interface-capturing reconstruction only to variables and wave families for which it is physically appropriate, while central or low-dissipation reconstruction protects continuous tangential velocity and vortical content. This suppresses spurious artifacts while preserving sharp interfaces on relatively coarse grids.

Representative work: Wave-Appropriate Multidimensional Upwinding… (JCP, 2025); Physics Appropriate Interface Capturing Reconstruction… (Computers & Fluids, 2025); Fully Conservative and Semi-Conservative Eigenstructures (JCP, 2026).

05

From algorithms to usable tools

High-Performance Solver Development and Research Translation

The final stage translates the numerical research into working CFD software. My experience includes Fortran solvers using MPI and OpenACC as well as GPU-accelerated implementations in NVIDIA Warp.

The open-source WA-CR-Warp solver implements wave-appropriate reconstruction for three-dimensional compressible flow and has been exercised on large benchmark problems using NVIDIA A100 hardware. The software closes the loop from mathematical design to the performance, stability, and robustness demands of practical computational physics.

Open-source software: WA-CR-Warp on GitHub.

Previous projects

Hyperbolic and High-Order Methods for Magnetized Plasma and Anisotropic Diffusion

My doctoral research addressed low-temperature magnetized plasma flows for Hall thrusters and spacecraft propulsion. In these systems, electron transport is strongly anisotropic, and the numerical problem becomes especially difficult when a computational grid is not aligned with the magnetic field.

I developed high-order upwind and non-oscillatory methods for steady diffusion and advection–diffusion, together with a first-order hyperbolic formulation for anisotropic diffusion. The hyperbolic approach offered a robust alternative to conventional elliptic treatment under severe anisotropy and grid misalignment.

Representative work: First Order Hyperbolic Approach for Anisotropic Diffusion Equation (JCP, 2019); High-Order Upwind and Non-Oscillatory Approach… (JCP, 2018).
